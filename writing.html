Why VGGT-Omega didn't work for our light stage

EVALUATION NOTE · 2026

Our reconstruction pipeline is built on calibrated multi-view capture: 30 cameras and 9 projectors, calibrated to sub-0.2mm reprojection accuracy, feeding a custom distorted-camera-model pipeline that's been industrialized across two hardware generations. It's not a glamorous architecture. It's also the reason the geometry we ship to clients like Amazon, Meta, and Nike holds up under scrutiny.

Feed-forward reconstructors have gotten good fast, so periodically it's worth asking whether a learned, calibration-free approach could replace part of that pipeline. I ran VGGT-Omega against our own capture data to find out.

It didn't hold up. The geometry it recovered was plausible at a glance but fell short of the accuracy our production pipeline requires once you look closely at fine structure and metric scale. That's not a criticism of the model in general — it's a strong result on the benchmarks it was built for. It's a specific finding about the gap between benchmark-grade and production-grade for a use case with a tight accuracy bar and a capture setup (structured light, known camera/projector geometry) that a general feed-forward model isn't built to exploit.

The practical upshot: for now, our calibration-based approach stays in production, and the feed-forward literature stays something I track rather than something I've replaced anything with. I'd rather report that plainly than imply otherwise.

Estimating physical properties without a mesh

METHOD NOTE · 2026

Part of what I guide our PhD interns on is encoding mechanical material properties — things like Young's modulus and Poisson's ratio — directly into reconstructed geometry, so that a scanned object isn't just visually right but physically usable downstream (in simulation, in engineering analysis, wherever the asset ends up).

The classical way to estimate these properties involves building a volumetric mesh and solving a PDE (typically via finite element methods) over it. Meshing is often the expensive, fragile part — a poor mesh produces poor results no matter how good your solver is, and meshing complex or thin geometry reliably is its own research problem.

The Walk-on-Spheres line of work (associated with Rohan Sawhney and collaborators) sidesteps this: it solves the same class of PDE directly on the geometry, without ever constructing a volumetric mesh, using a Monte Carlo method that recursively samples spheres inscribed in the domain. It's a genuinely different way to think about the problem — geometry processing and PDE solving without the meshing step in between.

My own contribution to Zombie, the open-source project implementing this line of work, was building out its CI and testing infrastructure — not the numerical method itself. But it's the reason I spend time with this material at all: the mesh-free framing is directly relevant to the inverse-FEM direction I'm currently guiding our interns on, and it's the kind of geometry problem I'd like to spend more of my time on going forward.
